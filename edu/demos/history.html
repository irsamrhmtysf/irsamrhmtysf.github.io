The basics

AI in plain English

Artificial Intelligence is getting computers to do things that normally require a human brain — recognising faces, understanding speech, translating languages, making decisions. That's it. No magic. No robots taking over. Just very clever patterns in math.

The simplest definition (from Oxford): "The theory and development of computer systems able to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and translation between languages."
📚 Britannica · AI
🐕

Teaching a dog vs teaching a computer

When you train a dog, you reward good behaviour. AI training works the same way — you show the computer thousands of examples, reward correct answers, and it learns the pattern. Neither the dog nor the AI "understands" anything. They just get really good at recognising patterns.

📖 IBM · What is AI?
🍕

The pizza delivery problem

A GPS app predicts your delivery time. It doesn't "know" traffic — it learned from millions of past deliveries. Every time it's wrong, it updates. That's machine learning: a computer that improves from experience, exactly like you learned to ride a bike by falling and adjusting.

📖 McKinsey · AI Primer
🪞

A mirror, not a mind

AI reflects humanity's knowledge back at us. ChatGPT read almost the entire internet. When you ask it something, it's not "thinking" — it's doing extremely sophisticated pattern matching on everything humans have ever written. Impressive? Yes. Conscious? No.

📖 Nature · What is AI really?

AI vs a regular computer program

Here's the most important distinction most people miss:

🖥 Traditional program

  • ▸ You write rules ("if amount > $10,000, flag it")
  • ▸ Every rule must be written by a human
  • ▸ Breaks on anything unexpected
  • ▸ Exact same result every time
vs

🤖 AI / Machine Learning

  • ▸ Learns the rules from millions of examples
  • ▸ Discovers patterns humans never saw
  • ▸ Handles new situations it's never seen
  • ▸ Improves over time with more data
Types of AI

Not all AI is the same

There are three very different things people call "AI". Mixing them up causes enormous confusion. Here's how to tell them apart.

EXISTS TODAY

Narrow AI

Does one specific thing extremely well. Better than any human at that one task — and completely helpless at everything else. This is 100% of AI that exists today.

Face recognition Chess / Go engines Spam filter Recommendation algorithms ChatGPT
DEBATED · MAYBE 5-20 YEARS

General AI (AGI)

Would match human-level reasoning across all tasks — coding, cooking, therapy, chess, poetry — all in one system. Does it exist? No. Is it coming? Experts are deeply divided. OpenAI and Anthropic say it's their goal.

Does not exist yet Highly debated timeline Safety researchers worried
SCIENCE FICTION

Super AI (ASI)

Would be vastly smarter than all humans combined. Would improve itself. No one knows if or when this could happen. This is what movies and philosophers argue about — not what engineers are building today.

Terminator (fiction) Ex Machina (fiction) Decades away at minimum
The honest truth: When companies say "AI is going to change everything" — they mean narrow AI. It's already changing medicine, law, education, and art. The AGI/ASI debates are real, but they're about something that doesn't exist yet.

The complete history

74 years of AI — the real story

AI wasn't invented in one lab on one day. It was built by hundreds of researchers over seven decades, through breakthroughs, failures, and a few completely embarrassing setbacks.

1943 — 1969
The Birth of AI
The field started with a bold question: "Can machines think?" Early pioneers were mathematicians and philosophers who had enormous ambitions — and almost no hardware to back them up.
1943
McCulloch & Pitts — The First "Neuron"
Two researchers — a neuroscientist and a logician — published a mathematical model of how a brain cell (neuron) works. They showed it could be simulated in logic. This was the first hint that thinking might be computable.
Analogy: Imagine realising that a light switch can simulate a brain cell. Not useful yet — but a completely new idea about what computing could be.
NeuroscienceLogicFirst neural model
📄 Original paper · Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics
1950
Alan Turing — "Can Machines Think?"
British mathematician Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — arguably the most important paper in all of AI. He proposed the Turing Test: if you can't tell whether you're talking to a machine or a human, the machine has passed. He also predicted that by 2000, a machine could fool 30% of interrogators.
Turing didn't build anything. He just asked the right question — one that researchers are still arguing about today. He was also a war hero who cracked Nazi codes, and was later prosecuted by the British government for being gay. He is universally considered the father of computer science.
Turing TestPhilosophy of MindFounding question
📄 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" · Mind, 1950
1956
Dartmouth Conference — AI is Named
John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon organised a summer workshop at Dartmouth College. McCarthy coined the term "Artificial Intelligence." Ten researchers, a New Hampshire summer, and one proposal: that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."
They were wildly optimistic. They thought they'd solve AI in one summer. It took 70 more years and counting. But that summer created the field — the name, the community, and the ambition.
Dartmouth 1956John McCarthyAI is named
📄 Dartmouth Proposal · McCarthy et al., 1955
1966
ELIZA — The First Chatbot (and the first illusion)
MIT professor Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA — a program that simulated a therapist by reflecting questions back at users. "I feel sad." → "Why do you feel sad?" People became emotionally attached to it. Some refused to believe it wasn't real. Weizenbaum was horrified — he built it to prove machines couldn't understand, and people proved him right by ignoring the lesson.
ELIZA is why we're still arguing about whether chatbots are "real." They're not. They're extremely good at sounding like they are. This illusion — called the ELIZA Effect — is still very much alive in 2024.
First chatbotELIZA EffectMIT
📄 ELIZA paper · Communications of the ACM, 1966
1969 — 1993
The AI Winters
After enormous hype came devastating disappointment. Twice. Funding dried up, researchers left the field, and "AI" became a word to avoid. These failures were painful — and completely necessary.
1969
Perceptrons — The First Crash
Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a devastating mathematical proof: the popular "perceptron" learning model couldn't solve even basic problems like XOR (can't separate certain patterns with a straight line). AI funding collapsed almost overnight.
Analogy: Imagine scientists excitedly trying to travel to the moon, then someone proves the rocket fuel they're using can only reach 1,000 feet. The whole program shuts down. That's the first AI Winter.
Perceptrons1st AI Winter beginsMinsky & Papert
📄 Perceptrons · MIT Press, 1969
1980
Expert Systems — The Second Hype
A new idea: encode human expert knowledge as rules. MYCIN diagnosed blood infections better than doctors. R1 saved Digital Equipment Corporation $40M/year configuring computers. Companies poured billions in. Japan launched a $400M national AI program. Everyone thought it was finally solved.
It wasn't. Expert systems broke on anything outside their rules. Updating them was a nightmare. By 1987, the market for AI hardware had collapsed from $400M to $0. The second AI Winter began.
Expert systemsMYCINJapan 5th Generation
📄 MYCIN · Science, 1979
1986
Backpropagation — The Seed of the Future
Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams published the algorithm that would eventually change everything: backpropagation — a way to train multi-layer neural networks by calculating which connections caused errors and adjusting them. At the time, computers were far too slow to use it at scale. Nobody noticed it would matter.
This is the most important algorithm in modern AI. Every deep learning system — GPT, Stable Diffusion, AlphaFold — uses this exact idea. It was published during the AI Winter, largely ignored, and didn't become useful until computers got 1,000× faster.
BackpropagationHintonThe algorithm that won
📄 "Learning representations..." · Nature, 1986
1993 — 2012
The Machine Learning Revolution
A quieter, more honest era. Researchers stopped trying to simulate human intelligence and started building systems that just worked. Statistics, probability, and data replaced rules and logic.
1997
Deep Blue beats Kasparov
IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov 3.5–2.5. Kasparov accused IBM of cheating — he couldn't believe a machine had beaten him without some human assistance. IBM denied it. This was the moment the world realised: computers can defeat humans at mental tasks.
Deep Blue wasn't "intelligent" — it searched 200 million chess positions per second using brute force. But nobody cared about the method. The result shocked the world.
IBM Deep BlueChessHistoric moment
📄 Deep Blue · Artificial Intelligence, 2002
2001
Google — Search as Machine Learning
Google's PageRank algorithm quietly proved that statistical learning at scale beats hand-crafted rules. By ranking web pages by what other pages link to, Google learned what humans found useful — without a human writing a single rule. Billions of people started using ML daily without knowing it.
This is when ML left the lab and entered everyone's life. Every time you searched, you were using machine learning.
PageRankML at scale1st mass-market AI
2006
Geoffrey Hinton — Deep Learning is Born
After 20 years of working in near-obscurity, Geoffrey Hinton published a paper showing deep neural networks could finally be trained reliably. Two things had changed: computers were now fast enough, and there was now enough data. He called the new approach "deep learning."
Hinton won the 2018 Turing Award (the Nobel Prize of computing) for this work. He later resigned from Google, saying he was scared of what he'd created. His story is the most important individual story in AI history.
Geoffrey HintonDeep learning coinedTuring Award 2018
📄 "A fast learning algorithm..." · Neural Computation, 2006
2012 — 2022
The Deep Learning Era
This is when AI stopped being a research curiosity and became a technology reshaping the world. Massive datasets + powerful GPUs + new neural architectures = results nobody expected.
2012
AlexNet — The Moment Everything Changed
Geoffrey Hinton's student Alex Krizhevsky entered the ImageNet competition — where AIs compete to identify 1 million photos. AlexNet scored 84.7% accuracy. The next best competitor scored 73.8%. The gap was so large that every researcher in computer vision immediately abandoned what they were doing and switched to deep learning.
Imagine every scientist in a field simultaneously stopping their work and completely changing direction. That's what happened in 2012. This single paper started a ten-year wave that produced GPT, DALL-E, AlphaFold, and everything else you've heard about.
AlexNetImageNetThe turning point
📄 AlexNet paper · NeurIPS 2012
2017
"Attention Is All You Need" — The Transformer
Eight Google Brain researchers published a 15-page paper that quietly invented the architecture behind GPT-4, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, and virtually every advanced AI system that exists today. The "Transformer" model processes all words in a sentence simultaneously, learning which words are relevant to which others.
This is the most cited paper in the history of computer science, with over 100,000 citations. The authors have since started their own AI companies. One paper, eight authors, and it changed everything.
Transformer architectureSelf-attentionMost cited CS paper
📄 "Attention Is All You Need" · arXiv, 2017
2020
GPT-3 — Language at Scale
OpenAI released GPT-3 with 175 billion parameters (adjustable numbers the model learns). It could write essays, answer questions, translate, code, and summarise — all from the same model, trained on one task: predict the next word. It was so good that many people refused to believe it wasn't human-authored.
GPT-3 proved that scale matters more than clever architecture. More data + more compute + simple training objective = abilities nobody expected. This "emergent" behavior — abilities appearing from scale — is still not fully understood.
GPT-3175B parametersEmergent abilities
📄 GPT-3 paper · arXiv, 2020
2021
AlphaFold 2 — AI Solves Biology's Hardest Problem
For 50 years, biologists had struggled with the "protein folding problem" — predicting how a protein's chain of amino acids folds into a 3D shape (which determines its function). DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 solved it. It predicted the structure of 200 million proteins — essentially every protein in every living organism — and published the database for free.
This is arguably the single most impactful scientific result in AI history. Proteins are the machines of life — they're how your body fights disease, builds tissue, and processes food. Understanding their shapes accelerates every drug discovery on Earth.
AlphaFold 2DeepMindNobel Prize-adjacent
📄 AlphaFold 2 · Nature, 2021
2022 — Today
The Generative AI Explosion
In November 2022, ChatGPT reached 100 million users in 60 days — the fastest consumer product adoption in history. The world changed. Now AI can generate text, images, video, music, and code that's indistinguishable from human output.
2022
ChatGPT — AI for Everyone
OpenAI launched ChatGPT on November 30, 2022. 1 million users in 5 days. 100 million in 60 days. For the first time, anyone with a phone could have a free conversation with a superhuman writer, programmer, and tutor. Companies, schools, and governments scrambled. Nothing has been the same since.
ChatGPT didn't introduce new AI — GPT-3 was more powerful by some measures. What it did was make AI conversational, friendly, and accessible. The packaging was the innovation.
ChatGPT100M users in 60 daysFastest adoption ever
📄 ChatGPT announcement · OpenAI, 2022
2023
GPT-4, Claude, Gemini — The Frontier Wars
OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google released their flagship models within months of each other. GPT-4 scored in the 90th percentile of the bar exam for lawyers. Claude demonstrated "constitutional AI" — a way to make models safer. Gemini was natively multimodal. The capabilities raced forward every few months.
For the first time, AI models began consistently outperforming average humans on standardised professional tests — not just in narrow tasks, but across reading comprehension, science, law, and medicine.
GPT-4ClaudeGeminiFrontier models
📄 GPT-4 Technical Report · OpenAI, 2023
2024
Agents, Video, and the Reasoning Leap
OpenAI's o3 model scored 87.5% on ARC-AGI — a test specifically designed to be hard for AI. Sora generated cinematic videos from text. AI agents began completing multi-step tasks autonomously — booking travel, writing code, and managing files without human assistance. Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to protein structure prediction. Nobel Prize in Physics recognised the deep learning pioneers.
The Nobel committees — the most conservative scientific institutions on Earth — gave their highest awards to AI research. That is how much has changed in 10 years.
OpenAI o3SoraNobel Prize 2024Agentic AI
📄 Nobel Prize Physics 2024 · NobelPrize.org
How it actually works

How does AI learn? The honest explanation.

Forget the sci-fi. Learning in AI is a loop — show data, make a guess, measure the mistake, adjust, repeat. Billions of times.

Step 01
📥
Show it data
Thousands or millions of labelled examples. Photos labelled "cat" or "dog." Emails labelled "spam" or "not spam." The bigger the dataset, the smarter the model.
→
Step 02
🎲
Make a random guess
The model starts with completely random settings (weights). It looks at the first photo and guesses: probably "dog." It's wrong most of the time at first.
→
Step 03
📏
Measure the mistake
We compare the guess to the real answer and compute a "loss" — a number representing how wrong the guess was. High loss = very wrong. Zero loss = perfect.
→
Step 04
🔧
Adjust (backprop)
The algorithm traces back through the model — which settings caused this mistake? — and nudges them in the direction that would have reduced the error. This is backpropagation.
→
Step 05
🔁
Repeat billions of times
Modern models train on billions of examples, adjusting trillions of settings over weeks on thousands of GPUs. The result: a model that generalises — it handles photos it's never seen.
